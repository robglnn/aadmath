- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.SSE.A.2 — what is NOT claimed.** The structure read here sits inside a whole number under a bar, not inside an algebraic expression. The polynomial half of this standard is carried by the factoring nodes.

## `complete-the-square`

> Half the middle coefficient, squared, is the number that turns a two-term start into a perfect square. Adding it to both sides keeps the balance and makes a squared bracket appear.

**Prerequisites.** `square-root-method`, `factor-trinomial-lead`, `fraction-solve`

| Framework | Code | 19 TAC | Depth | Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TEKS | `A.8(A)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(8)(A)` | core | solve quadratic equations having real solutions by factoring, taking square roots, completing the square, and applying the quadratic formula; and |
| TEKS | `A.6(B)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(6)(B)` | supporting | write equations of quadratic functions given the vertex and another point on the graph, write the equation in vertex form (f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k), and rewrite the equation from vertex form to standard form (f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c); and |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.SSE.B.3.B` | — | core | Complete the square in a quadratic expression to reveal the maximum or minimum value of the function it defines. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.REI.B.4.A` | — | supporting | Use the method of completing the square to transform any quadratic equation in x into an equation of the form (x - p)^2 = q that has the same solutions. Derive the quadratic formula from this form. |

- **A.8(A) — what is NOT claimed.** This node proves the completing-the-square route, including the case where the squared term carries a number that has to come out first. The other three methods are proved by the nodes around it.
- **A.6(B) — what is NOT claimed.** Vertex form is produced from standard form and proved. Writing a rule from a turning point and a second reading is the later node, and it is not asked here.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.SSE.B.3.B — what is NOT claimed.** The square is completed and the value at the turn appears in the answer. Naming which of maximum and minimum it is belongs to the features node.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.REI.B.4.A — what is NOT claimed.** The transformation into a squared bracket equal to a number is produced and proved. Deriving the formula from that form is stated in the next node and is never asked of the learner.

**What the gate proves.**

- *CCSS-M* — HSA.SSE.B.3.B is core: the answer must be one squared bracket plus a constant, and the checker refuses standard form even though standard form is identically equal. HSA.REI.B.4.A is supporting because its second sentence asks for a derivation, and a derivation is not a value an independent checker can re-derive.
- *TEKS* — A.8(A) core, completing-the-square route. A.6(B) is supporting here and core one node later, so a reviewer can see which half of that three-clause expectation each node carries.

**Process standards.** CCSS-M MP.7, MP.1, MP.8 · TEKS A.1(A), A.1(B), A.1(F)

**Misconceptions this node declares.** `half-b-not-squared`, `added-one-side-only`, `leading-coefficient-not-divided-out`, `bracket-sign-copied`, `constant-dropped`, `perfect-square-left-inside`, `plus-only`

## `quadratic-formula`

> Completing the square on a general squared rule gives one formula that works every time. The part under the root says how many real answers there are.

**Prerequisites.** `complete-the-square`, `solve-by-factoring`, `radical-arith`

| Framework | Code | 19 TAC | Depth | Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TEKS | `A.8(A)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(8)(A)` | core | solve quadratic equations having real solutions by factoring, taking square roots, completing the square, and applying the quadratic formula; and |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.REI.B.4.B` | — | core | Solve quadratic equations by inspection (e.g., for x^2 = 49), taking square roots, completing the square, the quadratic formula and factoring, as appropriate to the initial form of the equation. Recognize when the quadratic formula gives complex solutions and write them as a ± bi for real numbers a and b. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.CED.A.1` | — | supporting | Create equations and inequalities in one variable and use them to solve problems. |

- **A.8(A) — what is NOT claimed.** This node proves the formula route, including the reading of the part under the root that says there is no real answer. The other three methods are proved by the nodes before it.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.REI.B.4.B — what is NOT claimed.** The formula route is proved, on rational and on irrational answers alike. The complex-solution clause is outside this course: an equation whose part under the root is below zero is answered "no solution".
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.CED.A.1 — what is NOT claimed.** One form builds the equation from a thrown object before solving it. Most forms hand the learner the equation already written.

**What the gate proves.**

- *CCSS-M* — The irrational answers are the reason this node exists in a bank that refuses decimals. The checker works in exact arithmetic widened to include one square root, so a root such as 1 + the root of 5 is compared exactly and is then put back into the printed equation, which must come to exactly zero.
- *TEKS* — A.8(A) core, formula route. This closes the expectation: with this node held, all four of the methods it names have been proved, one node each.

**Process standards.** CCSS-M MP.7, MP.6, MP.8 · TEKS A.1(A), A.1(C), A.1(D)

**Misconceptions this node declares.** `minus-b-forgotten`, `discriminant-sign-slip`, `denominator-under-one-term-only`, `negative-discriminant-rooted`, `a-read-as-one`, `perfect-square-left-inside`

## `parabola-features`

> A squared rule draws a curve that falls, turns and rises again. The turning point, the line through it, the crossings and the highest or lowest value are all read from the same three numbers.

**Prerequisites.** `solve-by-factoring`, `graph-linear`, `function-notation`

| Framework | Code | 19 TAC | Depth | Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TEKS | `A.7(A)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(7)(A)` | supporting | graph quadratic functions on the coordinate plane and use the graph to identify key attributes, if possible, including x-intercept, y-intercept, zeros, maximum value, minimum values, vertex, and the equation of the axis of symmetry; |
| TEKS | `A.6(A)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(6)(A)` | supporting | determine the domain and range of quadratic functions and represent the domain and range using inequalities; |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.C.8.A` | — | core | Use the process of factoring and completing the square in a quadratic function to show zeros, extreme values, and symmetry of the graph, and interpret these in terms of a context. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.C.7.A` | — | supporting | Graph linear and quadratic functions and show intercepts, maxima, and minima. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.B.4` | — | supporting | For a function that models a relationship between two quantities, interpret key features of graphs and tables in terms of the quantities, and sketch graphs showing key features given a verbal description of the relationship. Key features include: intercepts; intervals where the function is increasing, decreasing, positive, or negative; relative maximums and minimums; symmetries; end behavior; and periodicity. |

- **A.7(A) — what is NOT claimed.** Every attribute the expectation names is produced from the printed rule and re-derived exactly: both crossings, the zeros, the greatest or least value, the turning point and the equation of the line of symmetry. The graphing clause is NOT met, because the renderer draws straight segments and isolated points only and cannot draw a curve.
- **A.6(A) — what is NOT claimed.** The range is produced as an inequality and proved by an identity that reproduces the printed rule. The domain is not asked: every rule of degree two accepts every real number, so the answer would be recall rather than a derivation.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.C.8.A — what is NOT claimed.** Factoring and completing the square are what produce the zeros, the value at the turn and the line of symmetry, and one form asks for them inside a situation. The graph itself is not drawn.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.C.7.A — what is NOT claimed.** The intercepts, the maximum and the minimum are produced exactly. No graph is drawn, and the standard asks for one.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.B.4 — what is NOT claimed.** Key features are read off a printed rule and off a table of readings. No graph is read and none is sketched, and the standard names both.

**What the gate proves.**

- *CCSS-M* — Only HSF.IF.C.8.A is core, and that is the one row here whose whole subject is a process rather than a picture. Everything else in this node is cited at supporting with the drawing named as the missing part, because this product cannot draw a parabola and a claim that says otherwise would be found in five minutes by anyone who opened it.
- *TEKS* — A.7(A) is the most attractive over-claim in the strand and it is refused. Every attribute the expectation lists is a finite quantity that the checker re-derives exactly, and the bank asks for all of them; but the expectation opens with "graph quadratic functions on the coordinate plane and use the graph to identify", and this node never draws the graph. A.6(A) is supporting for a different reason, and the caveat says which half is missing. The non-symbolic reading is a TABLE of readings whose rule the checker recovers by exact least squares, not a picture.

**Process standards.** CCSS-M MP.2, MP.7, MP.4 · TEKS A.1(A), A.1(D), A.1(E)

**Misconceptions this node declares.** `turning-point-sign-wrong`, `constant-called-the-turn`, `axis-given-as-a-number`, `range-written-about-the-input`, `highest-and-lowest-swapped`, `constant-rate-assumed`, `swapped-roles`

## `quadratic-from-vertex`

> A squared rule can be written with its turning point showing, or as the product of the two brackets that are zero where the rule is. The two spellings describe the same curve.

**Prerequisites.** `complete-the-square`, `parabola-features`

| Framework | Code | 19 TAC | Depth | Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TEKS | `A.6(B)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(6)(B)` | core | write equations of quadratic functions given the vertex and another point on the graph, write the equation in vertex form (f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k), and rewrite the equation from vertex form to standard form (f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c); and |
| TEKS | `A.6(C)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(6)(C)` | supporting | write quadratic functions when given real solutions and graphs of their related equations. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.SSE.B.3.A` | — | supporting | Factor a quadratic expression to reveal the zeros of the function it defines. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.CED.A.2` | — | supporting | Create equations in two or more variables to represent relationships between quantities; graph equations on coordinate axes with labels and scales. |

- **A.6(B) — what is NOT claimed.** Two of the three clauses are proved with the turning point given: the rule is written in vertex form, and a rule in vertex form is rewritten to standard form. Building a rule from a turning point and a second reading ALONE is not asked, because such a prompt shows no rule for the checker to read.
- **A.6(C) — what is NOT claimed.** The learner is given the real solutions and writes the rule as a product of two brackets. The graphs half of the expectation is not met, because no curve is drawn.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.SSE.B.3.A — what is NOT claimed.** A rule is written as a product whose brackets are its zeros, in both directions. Revealing the zeros of an unfactored expression is the factoring nodes’ gate.

- *TEKS* — A.6(B) is core and the caveat is precise about which clause is missing and why. The expectation lists three things: write the equation given the vertex and another point, write it in vertex form, and rewrite it from vertex form to standard form. The last two are proved. The first is refused, because a prompt that carries only two points carries no notation an independent checker can re-derive an answer from, and this unit ships no item it cannot re-derive.

## `quadratic-model`

> A thrown object, a fenced area and a price against takings all follow a squared rule. The turning point is the greatest height, the largest area or the best price, and the zeros are where the story ends.

**Prerequisites.** `quadratic-formula`, `parabola-features`

| Framework | Code | 19 TAC | Depth | Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TEKS | `A.6(C)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(6)(C)` | supporting | write quadratic functions when given real solutions and graphs of their related equations. |
| TEKS | `A.7(A)` | `19 TAC §111.39(c)(7)(A)` | supporting | graph quadratic functions on the coordinate plane and use the graph to identify key attributes, if possible, including x-intercept, y-intercept, zeros, maximum value, minimum values, vertex, and the equation of the axis of symmetry; |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.B.4` | — | core | For a function that models a relationship between two quantities, interpret key features of graphs and tables in terms of the quantities, and sketch graphs showing key features given a verbal description of the relationship. Key features include: intercepts; intervals where the function is increasing, decreasing, positive, or negative; relative maximums and minimums; symmetries; end behavior; and periodicity. |
| CCSS-M | `CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.CED.A.1` | — | supporting | Create equations and inequalities in one variable and use them to solve problems. |

- **A.6(C) — what is NOT claimed.** A squared rule is read inside a situation, and its greatest value, its best input and the time it reaches zero are produced. Writing the rule from stated solutions is the node before this one, and no graph is drawn.
- **A.7(A) — what is NOT claimed.** The key attributes are produced inside real-world problems, which is what the expectation asks them to be used for. No graph is drawn.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.IF.B.4 — what is NOT claimed.** The key features of a printed rule and of a table of readings are interpreted in the quantities of the situation. No graph is sketched, and the standard names sketching as well.
- **CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSA.CED.A.1 — what is NOT claimed.** The landing forms build an equation from the story and solve it. The reading forms are handed the rule already written.

**What the gate proves.**

- *CCSS-M* — HSF.IF.B.4 is the one core row, and it is core because interpretation in context is the whole node: every item names a quantity — a height, an area, a price, a time — and the answer is that quantity. The sketching clause is named in the caveat and is not claimed.
- *TEKS* — Both TEKS rows are supporting and both caveats name the drawing. The most useful thing this node does for A.7(A) is the part the expectation calls "in mathematical and real-world problems": the attributes are asked for as the greatest height, the largest area and the best price rather than as a vertex, so a learner who can find a turning point and cannot say what it means does not clear the gate.
